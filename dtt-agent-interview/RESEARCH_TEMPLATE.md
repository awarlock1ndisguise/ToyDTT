## 1. What DTT is

Discrete Trial Training (DTT) is a form of teaching in Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) therapy that breaks complex skills down into smaller understandable steps. This type of method has come in handy and has become a very well-researched area of autism treatments. A trial is considered "discrete" because it has a clear start, with a specific response and a consequent for any answer given from the participant. 

## 2. Sources used

1. Frank-Crawford et al. (2024): (Peer-reviewed) Provided foundational data on trial mechanics, prompt topography and intertrial intervals.
2. Altun & Yucesoy-Ozkan (2024): (Peer-reviewed) Provided more in-depth, the types and differences of different error correction mechanics used in DTT.
3. Ferguson et al. (2020): (Peer-reviewed) Provided trial-by-trial data logging protocols and introduced a new set of patient responses with their own unique reinforcements.


## 3. The elements of a trial

All three sources agree that a discrete trial consist of these components :
- Discriminative stimulus: The antecedent instruction starting the trial.
- Prompt: Assistance provided alongside the stimulus or after an incorrect answer is given from the learner.
- Response: The learner's action.
- Consequence: Feedback based on the learner's response (correct or incorrect).
- Break (Inter-Trial interval) - A brief break that separated one trial from the other.

## 4. Responses and how they are handled

- Correct response : All sources agree that correct responses are given reinforcement such as praise, "increasing the likelihood of learning" (Altun and Yucesoy-Ozkan 2024, 304). Frank-Crawford et al. mentions consumable reinforcement such as food or access to an activity.
- Incorrect response : Sources state that for incorrect answers interventionists provide corrective feedback like Error Correction(EC). Altun & Yucesoy-Ozkan went more in depth on the types of EC such as vocal feetbacks, A brief delay, multiple response repetition etc.
- No response : In all sources such a response has the same reinforcement as an incorrect response. In Remove and Represent (RRp) which is part of EC, a no response would be recorded as an incorrect answer which would have the researcher recording the child ignore him for a short time and then provide a prompt for the correct answer (Altun and Yucesoy-Ozkan 2024, 300).

## 5. Procedural topics

- Prompting - Frank-Crawford et al. outlines vocal, model and physical guidance prompts used in a trial in order to assist a learner.
- Prompt fading - Frank-Crawford et al. states that increasing or decreasing the level of prompt depends on the assistance they are trying to give across trials. 
- Reinforcement - All sources require there to be some type of reinforcement after each performance.
- Error correction - Altun & Yucesoy-Ozkan gives detail specific routes on multiple types of EC such as single-response repetitions, re-present until independent and more. 
- Movement between trials - Frank-Crawford et al. defines the intertrial interval as a few seconds between the ending of one trial and the beginning of the other.
- What is recorded per trial - Ferguson et al. records logging correctness, prompt levels used and the time gap between stimulus and response.
- When a session ends - All sources stay silent on the procedures after the session ends, instead having their main focus on the trial execution.

## 6. Where sources disagree

Ferguson et al. strictly separates correct answers between Independent Correct response vs Prompted correct response because they're different responses and are recorded as such.
On the other hand, Altun & Yucesoy-Ozkan and Frank-Crawford et al. don't distinguish correct answers between each other and every correct answer falls under the same category and same reinforcement.
An engineer following Ferguson et al. cannot use a binary state model (correct/incorrect) instead they need to calculate for each independent or prompt correct answer their own unique reinforcements.

## 8. Limitations

- Behavioral Adaptability: Working with a patient with autism for example, can be very unpredictable on how the trials can affect them emotionally. A computer cannot manage these challenging behavioral escalations.
- Rigid Prompting Rules: Human researchers can change and add prompts according to the clients' state while an agent relies on rigid, precoded rules that cannot adapt to the situation at hand.
- Tone: Computers lack vocal pitch, which risks the learner becoming confused on the monotone flat praises instead of a high pitch positive feedback from a human researcher.
